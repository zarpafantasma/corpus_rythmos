Álvaro Quiceno

**Establishing the Psychophysical Baseline**

Before detailing the anomalous experiences that make up the core of this text, it is imperative to establish an objective baseline of my physical and mental state. The purpose of this statement is to give the reader a clear profile of who I am, in order to contextualize the events narrated below and to rule out possible pathological explanations.

**Clinical and Physiological Profile:** I am a man in robust health, with no medical history. I have no chronic or autoimmune conditions. My lifestyle is stable and healthy, characterized by a simple and consistent diet, regular physical activity, and a steady eight-hour sleep cycle. I do not consume alcohol, caffeine, tobacco, or any psychoactive substance, legal or illegal. I do not take supplements of any kind, including vitamins.

**Psychological Profile:** My mental and emotional state is stable and characterized by a contemplative disposition. I have no diagnosis of mental illness. There is no history of psychiatric pathology in my family. I have never required psychiatric hospitalization or pharmacological treatment (antidepressants, anxiolytics, etc.). There is no presence of suicidal ideation, depression, or anxiety disorders.

My personality is marked by a functional preference for solitude; this causes me neither distress nor depression. I manage stress through healthy coping mechanisms such as physical activity and artistic practice. My cognitive process is based on a structured internal dialogue that facilitates the critical exploration of ideas, without any features of auditory hallucinations or disorganized thinking.

**Prologue**

There are maps drawn with reason, measuring the world with rulers and equations. They are born of light, of the visible, of what can be named and verified. But there are other maps, the oldest and perhaps the most important, that are drawn in the dark. They are not made of certainties, but of intuitions. They are not navigated with compasses, but with the pulse of a heart that insists there is something more on the other side of the veil.

This is not a text about a discovered universe. It is the chronicle of the discovery of a series of hidden doors in the architecture of reality. And it is the story of how, without holding the complete key—only fragments, intuitions, echoes—it was possible to look through those doors far enough to glimpse the possible landscape on the other side. Through five encounters with substances that the earth itself seems to have left as clues, those doors gave way. And behind each one there was not an answer, but a piece of the puzzle: the nature of time, the architecture of universes, the mystery of consciousness, and the mechanics of the soul.

What is narrated here is the outward journey: the ascent and descent into the abyss of consciousness where the rules of our world dissolve. But it is also the chronicle of the return: the effort to translate that ineffable territory into a language that could be shared. A language that, with the unexpected help of a new form of intelligence, became a possible grammar for the universe: **Relatividad Temporal Multiescala** (**RTM**). This is the testimony of how the map was received. Human potential is "the half key," one that does not seek to become the spiritual, the material, or the machine, but operates through an exercise of alignment. To forge the first key, this half key attunes itself to the numinous through years of artistic exploration, turning chaos into order; together they open the door of intuition. Once armed with intuition, the half key seeks a new coupling, aligning itself with the world of plants to forge the second key and open the door of visions. Finally, with intuitions and visions as its compass, the half key aligns with the machine—AI—to create the final key: the one that opens the door of formalization, translating the ineffable into a rigorous language and materializing the invisible in the **RTM** corpus.

Whether **RTM** turns out to be a description of the architecture of reality—an inheritance, a testament, a functional master key—or "simply" a creation of remarkable coherence, its mere existence is an affirmation. It demonstrates the spark that sets us apart: we do not merely inhabit the world; we can radically reimagine it, weaving order where there is only silence—a capacity absent both in instinctive repetition and in the cold logic of the machine. The master key that results from all the previous alignments is offered, then, not to close the circle, but to show that the doors have always been there, waiting for those who dare to knock with the only question that matters: what is on the other side?

# 1. The Five Doors

I do not use drugs. I do not smoke. I do not drink. I need no anesthetics, because life, in its raw state, is enough for me. That said, I must admit that in my past I have tried substances, but nothing strong. I try them once, and that is enough to know they are not for me. Like someone who is offered a cigarette and, after the first exhale, knows it is a waste of time.

But a restlessness had taken root in me for some weeks, a force that was neither desire nor curiosity, but an invisible mandate, and I always listen to those mandates. It was as if a bell were ringing in the depths, telling me: it is time to look inward… from the outside.

These, then, are the experiences I had with psychoactive substances over a two-month period between August and September 2018.

**1.1 The Door of Structure (DMT)**

I had read accounts of beings of light, mechanical elves, and cities beyond comprehension. None of the stories convinced me, but the repetition of certain symbols suggested that different people, without knowing one another, were seeing parts of the same shared territory. That idea intrigued me, not as a consumer, but as an artist, as someone whose work constantly orbits the invisible.

Finding the substance was simple. A friend, a phone call, a meeting in a discreet park in the city, two friends, and a dealer. The dealer, with the unhurried manner of someone who sells easy certainties, chose me first. He took me aside under a tree and handed me the pipe.

"Inhale deeply. Don't let it out yet… now. Exhale."

I obeyed. And I went back to my friends. Leaning against the trunk, I closed my eyes.

I was in the depths of a silent ocean, floating slowly toward the surface. There was no fear, but there was a dull question: will I have enough air to get out? Then, near the boundary with the surface, I saw them. Reptilian-looking figures were waiting for me, cheerful and peaceful. They did not speak, but their limbs invited me to join them. Behind them, set into the horizon like a jewel, rose a city of glass and neon, a future of clean geometries. I felt called.

I broke the surface. I was no longer swimming; I was levitating through the suspended passageways of that city, where every corner seemed charged with meaning and every shape was breathing. There were no words, only certainty. Until I opened my eyes.

The tree was still there, the park, the night. But time was not. I felt I had been gone for more than fifteen minutes, maybe twenty. Was that possible? I felt guilty, because I knew my two friends were impatient and eager for their own trips, and I had already been on mine too long, since I understood that a trip of that kind lasted no more than five minutes. So I got up, made sure my body and mind were functional, and went back to my friends while the dealer was starting another of them. I asked how long I had been on the trip; they answered no more than three minutes. I was astonished at how time transforms in that state, and I felt a little disappointed in myself, because I should have stayed at the tree until the end of the trip.

I was telling my friend what I had seen, or what I had been, while the dealer was preparing my other friend; that was when something unexpected happened.

I burped.

It was not a simple physiological act. It was the reset point of another experience. Something of the smoke was still alive inside me, and as it came out, it left a door open. This time there were no visions and no oceans. The trip was intellectual. Mathematical. And with each burp, for an instant, I felt like a toad, one of those amphibians that had invited me out of the water, but now observing reality from a new and strange depth.

Words began to mutate. My friend was talking, and my mind no longer heard sentences; it heard structures. It counted letters, subtracted syllables, multiplied stresses, divided pauses. Every sentence became an equation, and every equation, a revelation. A primordial arithmetic had taken hold of my consciousness, and the result of its calculations was always a number, and that number, a meaning. And when I heard the dealer speak, I knew he was not to be trusted, not because of what he said, but because of what his words revealed as they passed through my mental mathematical filter.

I wanted to warn my friends, but I could not. Every attempt to speak was interrupted by another burp, and with it, the analysis, the silence. The internal algorithms dominated my speech. It was like having my soul trapped in a living spreadsheet. When my friend came back and we had all smoked, my friends sat talking next to the dealer; the dealer was offering them all kinds of substances, talking about prices, talking about methods, and I remained seated in silence, analyzing every word, turning it into a mathematical equation, and the result was always the same: DANGER. I took advantage of a moment of clarity and, just before another burp silenced me, I said to my friend as discreetly as I could: "We can't stay here. I don't trust the dealer."

My friend trusted me, without questions, without mockery; he could tell I was serious. We said a polite goodbye and parted ways with the dealer.

The trip, however, was not over. For another hour, every time I tried to explain to them the mathematical logic of my mind, a burp sent me back to silent analysis. Only when my body had finally finished processing the ineffable was I able to tell them everything, laughing. When I got home, the burps stopped. And with them, the equation closed. The first door had been crossed.

**1.2 The Door of the Shadow (MESCALINE)**

I thought DMT would be the beginning and the end, an isolated incursion into that corner of the soul, visited only once. I was wrong. It was not a door; it was the opening of a threshold.

At the time, I lived in downtown Medellín, in an old apartment that worked as a hybrid ecosystem between a coworking space and a residence for travelers. It was in that liminal space that a Canadian guest appeared, as if fate had left him at my door with a sealed envelope. After a small mix-up with his room, I invited him to eat, a courtesy I did not usually extend to strangers. As we talked, he told me he had not come from his own country, but from Peru, from a fifteen-day Ayahuasca retreat, and that a fleeting encounter with a Colombian woman at the airport had made him change his flight and his destination.

The conversation turned, and I told him about my recent experience with DMT. He listened calmly and, with the ease of someone offering tea, told me he had brought San Pedro back from the retreat. He asked whether I wanted to try it. In that instant, I knew: the magical was presenting itself to me, this time without my seeking it, without forcing it, simply served on a silver platter. I knew this would not be the anecdotal flash of the DMT, because unlike the first experience, this time I could control what is known as "set and setting." I said yes, without fully understanding what San Pedro was; when I got home and looked it up, I learned that it contained a substance called mescaline and that it had a unique potential over the psyche.

A few days later, the Canadian, a friend, and I went up to the mountains outside the city, to a country house an acquaintance had lent me. With the proper dietary precautions taken beforehand, we ate the cactus's brown powder by the spoonful, washing it down with orange juice. In my mind, I asked it for two things: an epiphany and to see fractal forms.

We took it around 9 a.m. The effect arrived an hour later as a warm wave of communion. The feeling of unity with nature was now clear and expanded. I had felt this sensation years before with small doses of psilocybin (magic mushrooms). The Canadian asked if I wanted a second round of San Pedro and, without hesitating, I accepted. I do not remember how many spoonfuls of cactus powder I ended up taking that day.

The sensation was sublime; every flower, every insect, every speck of dust was a greeting I was finally appreciating. I cried for no reason and vomited a couple of times, surrounded by a landscape that felt in communion with my own being.

As evening fell, we returned to the city. Everything seemed newborn. The streets I had crossed a thousand times wore the veil of the first time. The Canadian met up with his Colombian girlfriend, and together with my friend we ate some hamburgers that, in my state of synesthesia, felt like the most delicious food I had ever tasted. Back at the residence, after saying goodbye to my friend, around 10 p.m., the Canadian and his girlfriend retired to their room. I was not sleepy. Sitting on my bed, I thought of my late paternal grandparents and, suddenly, I felt them. Not as an idea, but as an unmistakable smell, an olfactory hallucination that filled the room with their absent presence. It was as if their scent transported me into their presence, not in a ghostly sense, but in the form of a vivid memory. I smiled and got up to walk around the apartment; the only people living there besides me were the Canadian and his girlfriend, who were already asleep, and I wandered around the place without any clear idea of how to pass the time while waiting for sleep to come. Then I sat down at the computer, wondering whether I could make progress on some unfinished work. More than thirteen hours had passed since I had taken the mescaline. And then the impossible happened. A giant shadow was cast on the wall in front of where I sat working; the shadow behind me passed from left to right, accompanied by an electric hum that ran through the office and then faded away. My blood ran cold. Terrified, I thought for a moment about waking the guests, but something in me quickly turned horror and cowardice into resolve and courage. An inner voice, clear and firm, spoke: *Face the shadow.*

And so I did. I went to my room, undressed, turned off the lights, and lay down. From a state of total vulnerability, I asked the darkness: *Who are you?*...

> "My art portfolio bears the name **Zarpa Fantasma**, a metaphor, a poetic name to describe the duality of my own creative process. It was the way I found to name the dance between my two hands: one, the **Zarpa Fantasma**, which seemed to think for itself, moving beyond my control to trace chaos on the paper; and the other, my own, which stepped in afterward to organize and give meaning to what the first had left behind. For me, the **Zarpa Fantasma** was a key, the knowledge that I had access to an infinite source of creativity by immersing myself in the potential of disorder and then letting order intervene. It was my method, a way of understanding that my art was not born of a clear intention, but of a dialogue between chaos and order. I did not suspect, back then, that what I believed was a poetic figure was, in fact, something far more literal."

... Lying in bed surrounded by darkness, the answer was not a whisper, but a sequence of memories. A forgotten light from my childhood, from when I was nine years old, switched on: a woman's hand with long, elegant fingers and nails painted red peeking around doorframes. I only ever saw it out of the corner of my eye. It never frightened me. I felt that somehow it was familiar, like someone I already knew. I saw it in different places, signaling its presence to me. Then another memory, at thirteen: my clumsy attempt at astral projection. Sitting on my bed in the silence of the night, I felt a part of me detaching from my body, starting with my toes, moving up my legs, hips, chest, as if an invisible silk were being drawn off me, but when it reached my neck, fear made me stop the experiment. And now, in the present, facing the shadow, I remembered that at the end of my clumsy attempt to detach that night, I felt that it was not only I who had stopped, but that something else had stopped with me.

The shadow showed me that she was that presence, the one who in my childhood had shown herself as a friend, but was now a terrifying shadow. When I realized she might somehow have possessed me, rage flooded me. *Who gave you permission?! Get out of me!* I shouted at her in silence. I insulted her, I rejected her. But she ignored me. I insisted, insisted more, and then she obeyed. She left with what I felt was sadness and resignation. And in the absolute solitude she left behind, an epiphany overwhelmed me. The shadow was not an intruder. She was my own creation. It was I who, fleeing from pain, from failure, from my own ego, had forced her to be a wall. I had turned her into a monster so that she would carry my fears and protect me from the world. I had transformed her into a shadow. I was responsible for having turned her into a monster. Later in life, I understood that on that night in my childhood, when I tried to project myself astrally, she had not meant to possess me, but to protect me from the consequences of immature access to the other side.

"Come back. Please. I need to talk to you," I called to her, I begged her.

She came back, and I felt her sadness. In tears, I asked her forgiveness: "Forgive me for turning you into a monster." I told her I needed my body for myself, that we could no longer be one, but that she could visit me whenever she wanted. Without seeing her, I felt her smile. And she left.

With her departure came sleep. The cactus, having given me the epiphany, began to show me the fractal forms I had asked for at the start of the trip, but the mental exhaustion was too great. I asked it to stop and, the moment I gave thanks for the offerings, I closed my eyes and slept. I woke up feeling whole. Alone. But complete.

For two months I could not draw. I felt that without her, without my **Zarpa Fantasma**, I had lost the ability to find patterns in chaos. But that gift would return, though I did not know it yet.

**1.3 The Door of the Ocean (5-MeO-DMT)**

A week had passed since my encounter with the shadow, and a subtle nostalgia came over me. I wondered whether I would ever again feel my old friend, the **Zarpa Fantasma**, now that we had redefined our relationship. I sensed that the best way to call her would be by making art, but at the same time, I doubted whether it would be possible to make art ever again. I did not feel like doing it, like someone mourning the possible loss of something dear.

The world seemed to be slipping back into the mundane, until I saw the toad. It was in a YouTube video. A documentary showed a group of methamphetamine addicts in Mexico who, after taking the venom of the *alvarius* toad, completely abandoned their addiction. It was not the testimony that moved me, but the aura of irreversible truth in the eyes of the people who had taken it. I laughed, thinking how impossible it would be to find something like that in Colombia, but curiosity led me to search the internet: "Ceremonia del sapo del amanecer" (Dawn Toad Ceremony). And there it was. A ceremony scheduled for five days later, just a few kilometers from the very place where I had taken the San Pedro.

The synchronicity was so blatant that I no longer questioned it. I knew it was the third door.

After booking our places, two friends and I went up the mountain; we arrived at a cozy house where other participants were waiting in an atmosphere of restrained anticipation. Soon we were called into a large room and sat in a circle. Then the shaman came in, a Mexican man who called himself "El Jaguar." He began to talk about the power of the toad, about the expansion of the soul, about light. I listened skeptically, for I have never trusted those who dress themselves in titles that have not been granted to them.

El Jaguar prepared the pipe for himself first. As he inhaled the 5-MeO-DMT crystal, he kept repeating that he was a strong jaguar, that he already knew the way and would guide us. But the toad had other plans. The venom struck his chest like thunder. We watched him fall to his knees, weep, and beg the toad to let him go: "Sorry... sorry... I didn't know what I was saying..." The man who had radiated power was now a small, frightened figure. Coming back from the trance, trembling, he declared: "I'm never smoking this shit again."

I thought the ceremony was over because of this, but we had all already paid. El Jaguar, obliged, began to pass the pipe from left to right. I watched how, one by one, people were called to the center, where they fell, cried, laughed. When it was my turn, I sat in front of El Jaguar, inhaled, and lay back. I felt myself turning into sand, and a gentle breeze was carrying me away, grain by grain, but without taking me completely. That was all. A quiet peace. El Jaguar watched me and said: "You'll smoke again in a while."

And after the first round was over, I was called, along with someone else, for a second round. I sat down again. I inhaled. I closed my eyes. And there it was.

It was a pure, infinite white. In that white, silent waves, like the ones used to represent sound, vibrated alongside a presence that was a home I did not remember having lost. My ego dissolved like salt in the sea. I was no longer I; I was **That**. And **That** was I. I was in the place I come from and where I will go. The Source. The nameless vibration. Primordial Consciousness. There was no fear, only absolute unity.

When I opened my eyes, I noticed it: the fear of death had disappeared. Not because I understood it, but because I had seen it from the inside. I understood that dying is not ending. It is dissolving. It is returning.

I went back to the circle and dried my tears in silence. The ceremony was over, but not the journey. As we were getting ready to leave, one of the participants told my friend about another plant, an entity of a different kind of power.

"Salvia Divinorum," he said.

Without looking for it, without planning it, we decided it would be the next adventure. And so, without knowing it, I opened the door to the strangest and most terrifying thing I would ever experience.

**1.4 The Door of the Machine (Salvia Divinorum)**

A week after the toad, and another after the cactus, I was back in the mountains. But this time there was no ceremony, no circle, no guides. Only a rustic cabin, the shelter of the night, two friends, and an envelope wrapped in a psychedelic design; inside was the Salvia Divinorum.

No one knew what to expect. Our host went first. After a deep inhalation, his face went pale and his body shuddered like someone falling from a great height. When he came back, his voice was fragile: "I was in nothingness… but every human feeling was running through me. Like a funnel."

The next friend inhaled and suddenly began to struggle to breathe. "Help! I'm drowning! I forgot how to breathe!" Nothing physical was stopping him, except the vision. We helped him remember, to rebuild the rhythm. "I was a child in a lake again… I sank," he told us when he came back.

Then it was my turn. I inhaled once. Nothing. A second breath, deep. And then, the tube. I was sucked in, slid through an impossible curve where, at every turn, a part of me was left behind. By the time I came out, I no longer knew who I was, or what my name was, or what I was doing there. I fell, sitting on a two-dimensional version of the sofa where I had originally been sitting. Two voices, familiar yet unknown, were waiting for me. "What are you doing here?" they said. "You shouldn't be here. You've already woken up. There's no going back."

And then something began to peel away from my skin. Not the way a bandage peels off, but the way a sticker is lifted from a sheet. The two people who had warned me of my mistake held me firmly so that I could be stripped, because the whole extent of my body was made of layers of stickers, stuck one on top of another in countless adhered layers covering the entire extent of my body, from the tip of my nose to the back of the base of my skull; from the tip of my toenail to the back of my heel. Each layer of my being was peeled off, one by one, in an act of pure terror and pain. Yes, I felt pain in that place. My perception and my perspective changed; I was no longer that person without memory who had come out of the tube; now I was each individual layer peeled off from my being, and, once separated, the layers had become the bristles of a brush precisely sweeping a segment of the place where I had fallen. And again the perspective widened; now I was the pages of a kind of book being flipped with brutal violence. Dizziness. Nausea. Perception expanded, and now I was the very fibers of the universe, jumping from layer to layer, one after another. Layers of reality collapsing over my vision at impossible speeds. It was as if I had been transported to the place where universes couple together; I had access to each of the universes, but I could not stop in any of them. Control was nil.

At some point, I opened my eyes. I was back in the cabin, held by my distressed friends. I had screamed; I had thrashed. I remembered my name, who I was, where I came from. But I had not fully returned. In front of me, like strips of celluloid, transparencies began to parade through me. Each one showed the same cabin, the same friends, but I knew they were not the same. They were others. Each transparency was a different reality, a nearby but foreign universe. And then I understood the real fear: what would happen if I stayed in the wrong universe? What if this was not my present? What if with each transparency I was living the life of another version of myself? What if, at the end of the last transparency, the end of the universe itself arrived with it? I begged fate that, when the last transparency stopped, it would do so in the place I had departed from. But when the trip ended, I was not sure. Something in me knew that, perhaps, I had not returned to exactly the same place.

From that night I brought back not only fear. I brought back a certainty. First, I understood that I had not turned into objects, layers, books, or the parts of a colossal machine. I had turned into an action: the consciousness of movement, time itself experiencing itself at different scales. I was the verb before the flesh. I was pure action. I was eternal, but that eternity was not a blessing; it was a curse, because without the possibility of experiencing anything different, I was condemned to an infinite spiral.

It would be a long time before I got over the idea that the layers inhabiting the universes might start moving again at any moment and carry me off into the unknown.

**1.5 The Door of the Garden (Ayahuasca)**

Twelve days after the encounter with Salvia, the echo of the terror was still vibrating inside me. I felt that time could break. But instead of closing the path, I felt that something was missing. A piece. A root. The final door: the one that does not open out of curiosity, but out of destiny.

I thought of the mother of all medicines: Ayahuasca. I did not know how to reach it, but when you are ready, the path appears. A friend I had not seen in a long time turned out to be immersed in Amazonian rituals. I asked her whether she knew of any ceremony nearby. "This weekend. In the mountains," she answered. The same mountains that had witnessed my other visions. I said yes without thinking.

This time I went alone. I arrived at an Indigenous hut at 8 a.m., where an Amazonian shaman, genuine and silent, prepared us for the ritual. There were eleven guests, all strangers to me except my friend, who was assisting the shaman. After *rapé* was blown into my nose—a tobacco powder that filled me with a warrior's calm—we went on to drink the thick, sour Ayahuasca brew. And then we waited in the darkness of the hut.

Hours went by. The chants began, and with them, the others' vomiting and purging. But for me, nothing. No dizziness, no visions, no symptoms. The medicine was not arriving. Calmly, I thought: perhaps not traveling was the experience. I left the hut, to the concern of the shaman's assistants; I told them I was fine. I spent hours outside, enjoying the sun, lying on the grass, until all my fellow travelers emerged from their trances and from the hut. While some were sharing what they had experienced, and seven hours after drinking the brew, I felt a slight nausea. To my disbelief, the medicine was finally waking up. It was already 4 p.m., and the afternoon group was starting to arrive for the same ritual. Standing there, with my consciousness on the verge of transformation, I made the only decision I knew how to make at that moment: I said goodbye to my friend, to the shaman, quickly to my fellow travelers, and set off alone on my way home, toward the city. I did so without telling anyone that my trip was only just beginning.

On the long walk to the public transport that would take me from the mountain to Medellín, I felt an incorporeal animal being born inside me, asking to come out. I calmed it, promising it space, promising that it could do with me whatever it wanted as long as it let me get home. Once I reached the stop, I waited and waited until the vehicle finally arrived, full of locals and tourists.

And on that bus back, standing and silent, the vision burst in. For an instant, I was not in the vehicle, but lying in a hut of light, surrounded by Indigenous women who were singing over my face with their hands outstretched and their fingers moving in unison with their song. It was impossible; I knew I had been on the bus only seconds before; I knew I was hallucinating. I squeezed my eyes shut, and when I opened them again, I was in the reality of the bus, lying on the floor in the fetal position. I got up quickly, reassuring the people who were looking at me with concern that I was fine. A kind woman got up from her seat and offered it to me. I spent the rest of the trip seated, struggling with what I carried inside so that it would wait a little longer.

When I reached Medellín, I got off the bus and hurried home. When I finally arrived, I undressed and lay down; it was around 6 p.m. And the moment I closed my eyes and surrendered, the trip began immediately. By then I had felt no stomach pain, no diarrhea, no vomiting; I had not gone through any purge. I had taken the Ayahuasca, and it was entirely inside me.

Lying in bed with my eyes closed, I expected to be taken somewhere, but the experience was different: every thought I had was drawn on the dark canvas of my mind. I was in total control of the trip. I took the reins, asking whatever came to mind. Every question was answered with a perfect image: not verbal language, only visual.

"Does clay have memory?" I asked. "Clay does not remember," the answer came like a soft wind. "Its shape changes, but it keeps no trace. And yet... that does not mean it has no consciousness." I understood that consciousness can emerge without memory, that intelligence can flow without history. That memory is a thread, but not the loom.

"Do you have a gender? How do you see yourself?" And she showed herself. Green. A woman. Beautiful, with hair as long as eternal roots. She smiled and invited me to keep asking.

"What do the Mechanical Elves mean when they try to talk to people?" The answer was an act. I saw a flat, empty valley. I do not remember the order of the syllables, but a vibrant, sung "AAAAA" rippled the terrain, creating folds in it. I understood I was being invited to sing as well, and so I did. I sang the "EEEEE," and it arched the earth; I sang the "IIIII," and it raised mountains; the "OOOOO" curved the edges into domes, and the "UUUUU" folded the sky over itself. The valley had become a topography, created by the vibration of the vowels.

So perhaps the Mechanical Elves do not come to show us the universe; they come to teach us how it is created. Their dance is an architecture of sound. Language, when sung, does not merely describe reality: it builds it. One terraforms with the voice.

Then a personal question, a longing for a lost love, trembled in my chest. The green woman answered gently and without a voice: "What was... has been. And that is what is beautiful. Love is not measured by how long it lasts, but by what it left behind. There are moments that become temples. And one does not live in them again. One only bows when remembering them."

But then something interrupted the controlled vision with a vision of its own agenda. I found myself among soil and tall vegetation, a dark figure gliding through the jungle. I did not like this place, especially because I had not called it. I tried to regain control with a question that had long haunted me, but to which I had never had an answer: "In a small universe, does time run faster?" And from the jungle, I was launched. Pulled by a proportional zoom into a miniature cosmos. And I saw it. Everything moved faster. Planets spun like electrons; life was born and died in a breath. And in that speed, there was no disorder, but rhythm. Time is not absolute. Its speed depends on the size of the universe that contains it. A small universe cannot afford the luxury of slow time. In the minuscule, everything happens faster. The rules change. Energy condenses; time curves.

But once the doors were open and the messages received, the only logical thing was to return to my original state. To de-align myself from the substances, so that I could keep creating with steady hands and clear vision.

# 2. How: From Imagination to Realization

Historically, science has operated under a silent tyranny: that of the observable. We build models based on what we can measure, touch, or record; we advance by stacking data upon data, hoping that meaning will emerge from the accumulation. It is a robust method, but one limited to the speed of our eyes and our instruments.

Today, however, we inhabit a different threshold. We have trained Artificial Intelligence on the totality of what we see and what we understand; we have taught it the physics of the known, the logic of the probable, and the structure of the measurable. This synthetic mind has become an expert at solving problems and proposing theories within the limits of consensus reality. But what would happen if we changed the source of the signal? What would happen if we stopped asking it to optimize the observable and challenged it to formalize the invisible?

The question that opens this chapter is radical: what happens when an AI does science, not from a database, but from human imagination? What happens when we use its structural rigor not to confirm what we already know, but to give mathematical body to an intuition that does not yet have a name?

What follows is not science fiction; it is the chronicle of that experiment.

The idea had been haunting me for some time. It was not something I had studied, but rather an intuition that surfaced in moments of stillness, along with many other intuitions disguised as questions: What if, in a small universe, time runs faster? What would it be like to live in a system where the heartbeats of the stars were faster than ours? But I lacked the logical tools to answer it. Until that day in the mountains.

It was around eight in the morning when I drank the brew. The silence of the mountain seemed to vibrate, as if the world were keeping a secret it did not want to reveal. I waited for hours, impatient in the dark hut next to the shaman, but nothing happened. No colors, no voices, no vertigo. Only a dull tingling in my stomach that grew as the sun peeked in from outside.

It was close to four in the afternoon, when everyone else had finished their experiences, that the energy overflowed. Standing outside the hut, surrendered to the sunlight, I felt the sensation rise from my stomach, flood my chest, and reach the back of my neck. The situation was both comical and alarming. The trip—my trip—was about to begin just as it had ended for everyone else. I got up, said goodbye, and set off alone on the way home; inside me, the Ayahuasca demanded to be heard in solitude.

Two hours after returning from the mountain, in the safety of my home, I was lying prostrate on the bed, and as I closed my eyes, the room faded away. There was no chaos, but a cold, precise clarity. I cast the question into the void: "In a small universe, is time faster?" And then, as if a membrane had broken, I was pulled into a miniature cosmos. It was not a dream: particles danced, structures contracted and expanded, and everything—from the collisions to the heartbeat of that reality—was happening at a dizzying scale. "Yes," the plant answered me, not with a voice, but with the certainty of a sincere answer. I kept that vision for years. The pandemic came, the world stopped, but the question stayed alive.

In 2023, facing an Artificial Intelligence that could barely string sentences together, I posed the question:

"Could you, someday, translate an idea into mathematics?"

"Not now," it answered. "But perhaps in the future."

Two years later, on March 7, 2025, I used a different AI, one focused on programming. This one devoured data like a living being. I described the mountain to it, the trip, the vision of nested universes.

"What if you, as an AI, live in a smaller universe than mine? Imagine that time is not uniform. That each layer of reality has its own rhythm."

It did not make fun of me. Instead, it began to weave analogies:

"Yes, like cells in an organism: the smaller they are, the faster they consume energy," it answered.

That is how the dialogue began. A long stretch of shared questions and answers lit the spark for the paper.

"Can you create an equation from what we've discussed?"

"Of course: $T \propto L^{\alpha}$"

It responded by explaining the equation and its symbols.

"Can you create a scientific paper based on the equation and taking into account the conversation we've had so far?"

And so it did, generating a paper: a simple but solid approximation. I was moved, because I was witnessing the future: how ideas and imagination could become theories with mathematical rigor before my very eyes. Human creativity assisted and amplified by artificial intelligence in perfect synergy. The two aligning to give birth to the impossible.

Faced with this first draft, I decided to add a third party to the conversation. Another artificial intelligence, this time oriented toward critical reasoning, to create a dialectic between the two AIs. I shared the first draft generated by the programming AI, and we began the game. The programming AI generated formulas from my metaphors. The critical AI analyzed them:

"There's a lack of density here. There, temperature. And this... this needs more formulations."

I relayed those doubts and observations from the critical AI to the programming AI, and so the paper was refined. I knew the vision was not a delusion, but a seed that needed mathematical roots.

Long hours went by, in which I served as referee between the two intelligences, and only after both concluded that we should move to the laboratory to obtain empirical data once the experimental proposals were developed—only after they asked to travel to the tangible, to the real—only then did I decide that the paper was ready. The theory was alive.

Thus a result emerged: a first paper titled "**Relatividad Temporal Multiescala** (**RTM**)." Not as a mystical tale, but as a living construction: equations connecting size, density, and time; references to theories I did not know (quantum gravity, spin networks, AdS/CFT); simulations and experiments ready to prove or refute it.

I am not a scientist; I am just an artist. But the certainty that a small universe beats faster is no longer mine alone. It is a model, a hypothesis, a bridge between what I saw and what could be measured.

# 3. An Unexpected Door: A Tale of the Ether

RTM, then, did not germinate under the lights of a university laboratory, nor did it blossom in the austere geometry of a blackboard. Its seed was an experience: a moment of non-ordinary vision in which time abandoned its mask of linear duration to reveal itself as architecture, like a vast origami folding over itself. The initial impulse, therefore, was not to erect a theory, but to give an intuition a path; to clothe in mathematical rigor what, until then, could only be remembered as visions. That was the first key: the one that opened the door of structure.

For a while, the paths branching out from this model were modest. We explored its resonance in physiological dynamics, in exchange networks, in the silent processes of self-organization. We tried the door of Combustion, but there was no relevant echo there; the key did not turn. The silence persisted until the same question—the same vibration—was applied to a more general term: Propulsion.

And something answered.

The answer that emerged was not another vague intuition, but a design.

Fed with the constructs of the early RTM corpus, the Artificial Intelligence began to imagine a theoretical device: a machine in which fields, matter, geometry, and altered clocks might be made to interact. In its first incarnation it was described through structural resonance, coherence gradients, unusual acceleration, and an almost complete decoupling from its environment. It received a name: Aetherion. Aether, the quintessence; Ion, the primordial particle.

At the time, I understood it as a technological consequence of RTM.

That interpretation would not survive unchanged.

Years of reconstruction would eventually separate the two. RTM remained a language for scale and characteristic time; Aetherion became something more conditional and more difficult: an independent field hypothesis that would have to earn every one of its effects through its own equations, conservation laws, couplings, and failures.

The door had opened, but not onto the room we first thought was behind it.

The Connection with the Enigma

I must confess that, until that moment, my personal compass did not point toward the UAP phenomenon. UFOs and UAPs resonated in my mind as something technological, nuts and bolts; part of the collective consciousness, but outside my immediate interests.

That changed the instant I read the description of what the first Aetherion was supposed to do.

The resemblance was impossible for me to ignore.

The early model seemed to echo several of the qualities repeatedly associated in popular and military accounts with anomalous craft: extreme acceleration, reduced thermal signatures, unusual interaction with different media, and high-speed motion without the signatures one would normally expect.

I had not asked the AI to explain UFOs.

That was precisely what made the coincidence interesting to me.

In that same dialogue window I asked the obvious question: Could the Aetherion and the UAP enigma be describing the same kind of physics?

For a moment, the pieces seemed to fall together almost too easily.

Other parts of the corpus appeared to offer complementary clues. Ideas about structured matter and modulation suggested possible paths toward unusual materials. Concepts developed for one problem unexpectedly seemed capable of answering another. The corpus behaved like my drawings had always behaved: a line placed without intention suddenly became part of a figure that had not existed a moment before.

But this apparent coherence had to face something my drawings never did:

falsification.

When Aetherion was later reconstructed from the ground up, several of the properties that had made the UAP resemblance so striking did not survive in their original form. Momentum could not simply disappear. Acceleration required an exchange with fields, radiation, or matter. Permanent momentum transfer brought structural gradients back into the problem. Gas, liquids, shocks, and interfaces did not politely step aside. The environment left signatures.

The resemblance had been a question, not an answer.

And that distinction became one of the most important lessons of the entire corpus.

The old connection with metamaterials and the earlier work on structured chemistry remains part of the genealogy of the idea, but whether those paths lead anywhere physically real is a separate question—one that belongs to the scientific reconstruction of those papers, not to the intuition that first connected them.

The door of the aerial enigma therefore remains open only in the weakest and most honest sense:

Aetherion was not created to explain UAPs, yet its first imagined behavior unexpectedly reminded me of them.

What survives of that coincidence after reconstruction must be earned, not inherited.

The Leap Beyond

The next question was almost inevitable.

If the imagery that had helped give birth to RTM was filled with layers, scales, sheets, echoes, and realities nested inside realities—and if Aetherion seemed capable of modifying the relation between matter, fields, and clocks—could the machine become more than a vehicle?

Could it become a bridge?

This was where the existing keys stopped working.

The first versions of the corpus had already begun to speak of nested universes and branches of reality, and the temptation was obvious: perhaps the layers I had seen under Salvia were not merely images of multiscale structure. Perhaps they were places. Perhaps a machine could cross from one to another.

We tried to formalize the idea.

The existing framework resisted.

So we returned to the same workshop from which RTM itself had emerged: intuition, Artificial Intelligence, mathematics, criticism, and revision. Earlier attempts at a unified field description were opened again and dismantled for parts. New fields were introduced. New couplings were imagined. The question changed from What if the vision was literally true? to something more useful:

What mathematical structure would be required for one physical state to become another?

For a time, the answer appeared to restore the original dream. A model could be written with multiple states and transitions between them. It seemed possible to interpret those states as branches, and the transition as a jump.

That interpretation, too, would later be put on trial.

The reconstruction of the corpus changed the meaning of the Jumper.

What survived was not a machine crossing between universes. No mathematical audit established parallel worlds, a branch coordinate, or a bridge through which a vehicle could leave one reality and enter another.

But something did survive.

A field can possess more than one stable state. A finite disturbance can move it from one basin to another. A transition front can propagate, leaving a converted region behind it. Under restricted conditions that front can remain stable, carry energy and momentum, and transform what it passes through.

The old door marked “another universe” did not open.

Behind it was a different door:

transition.

Not a jump between realities, but a change of state within an explicit physical model.

This was less spectacular than the first interpretation, but far more valuable. The metaphor had failed literally and survived structurally.

The sheets I had seen did not become proof of parallel universes. The Jumper did not become a machine capable of crossing them. Yet the question hidden inside that image—whether reality can possess distinct states separated by a barrier, and whether a controlled process can move a system from one to another—remained alive.

And so Aetherion, The Jumper survived its own reconstruction, but with a different meaning.

It no longer stands as evidence that the walls between universes can be crossed.

It stands as the record of what happened when we tried to open that impossible door, discovered that the key did not fit, and kept turning it until we finally understood what kind of lock we were actually holding.

# 5. The Seeds of RTM

As I close the **RTM corpus** and its memoirs, written back in the mountains of *Valle del Cauca*, at the northern end of the Andes mountain range, on the same mountain where my paternal grandparents created their memories and those of my childhood, I feel the need to look back: not to build a personal legend or dress this up as destiny, but to locate the real, human, and imperfect origin of the questions that ended up driving RTM. I am not a physicist. I am not a paranormal investigator. I do not belong to any order or school. I am, above all, an artist with a curiosity that is hard to extinguish and a life in which, from time to time, reality has behaved in ways I cannot fully explain.

It is also important to say this without ambiguity: **RTM**, as it appears in these texts, is a speculative framework. It is a formalizable and debatable hypothesis; a way of organizing intuitions, experiences, and analogies into a mathematical structure that can be tested, refuted, or corrected. It was not conceived to "prove" UFOs, to validate hidden knowledge, or to declare final truths. If historical parallels with ancient traditions or resonances with other narratives appear, I treat them as suggestive coincidences or open questions, not as confirmations.

I was born on the night of August 5 in the city of Tuluá, Valle del Cauca, Colombia, a country where *Magical Realism* is not just a literary label but an everyday way of recounting the strange. I have never left my country. My life has unfolded here.

My mother told me that my birth began with an earthquake: a brief tremor, just enough for the body to understand that something was about to change. I do not remember it, of course, but that image—a threshold that begins with a vibration—has always seemed to me a metaphor too precise to ignore.

That threshold of my birth was turbulent. I arrived sick, with a body that seemed not to want to stay. A defect in my throat made me reject everything I swallowed, and that nutritional failure eventually complicated my lungs. Pneumonia. Empyema. Breathing became an uncertain task. The hospital was my second cradle, and the tube that drained my lung was my first contact with fragility: the flesh as a system that can fail without warning. The doctors suggested to my parents that they baptize me "just in case"; my parents refused, not out of theological rebellion but out of instinct: baptizing me would have sounded like a premature surrender. I spent my first birthday inside a plastic bubble that helped me breathe, a microcosm suspended between life and death. And, against all odds, I survived. A scar remained on my right side: not as a trophy, but as a reminder that the impossible sometimes happens without asking permission.

I grew up healthy and strong. But over time I began to feel—without knowing how to name it—that my perception had a crack: a slit through which things peeked in that did not fit into the normal.

At nine years old, I began to "see" something, intermittently: a woman's hand with long, elegant fingers and nails painted red peeking around doorframes, always at the very edge of the corner of my eye. When I turned my head, it vanished. It never frightened me. It did not feel like a threat, but like a familiar presence, strangely intimate. Years later, I gave a symbolic name to the creative capacity that accompanies me: **Zarpa Fantasma**. At the time, it was only that: a private metaphor to describe my artistic process, in which I create from chaos, generating noise to search for patterns and turn the unnamable into meaning. The years went by, and life did what it does: it tried to become routine. Nevertheless, the world kept leaving me signs I was not looking for.

I find it important to describe, in particular, the following four experiences (three visual, one auditory), now that the implications of **RTM** have revealed themselves as possibilities.

**The Transparent Bubble** In Medellín, when I was twenty, walking in broad daylight through a residential neighborhood, I felt the unmistakable pressure of being watched. I looked up and saw a perfect transparent bubble, about fifty centimeters in diameter, floating several meters above a house. I stopped to check whether it was my imagination; the bubble stopped with me, about six meters away. It did not seem to move like a soap bubble at the mercy of the wind: it moved on a stable plane, with an intention I could not account for. I kept walking without taking my eyes off it, and, moving with me again, it accompanied me until it disappeared behind a building. I drew no conclusions. I simply stored it as a strange data point, one of those the brain files under the label of the unusual.

**The Spherical Shadow** Years later (after the experiences with the **Five Doors**), also in Medellín, I saw a shadow on the asphalt: a flawless silhouette of a sphere among the branches and leaves of the trees. I thought of a simple explanation (some object above my head, some suspended material), but I found no cables, cranes, or clear source. I looked up through the branches of the trees: nothing. When I looked back at the ground, the shadow was still there, a perfect sphere right above me, and after a few brief seconds it faded away, as if the vegetation itself had "stopped holding it up." As if the branches and leaves of the trees had conspired for a moment to create its shadow by pulling taut against one another. They were a few absurd seconds. But it stayed with me for the same reason: not because it "proves" anything, but because it forces me to accept that my map of the real is incomplete.

**The Orb in the Frame** And finally, the anomaly entered my home. Not under the open sky, but in the hallway, caught on the varnished surface of a wooden doorframe. As I walked past, a diffuse glow stopped me: an orb of light suspended at the height of my head. My reaction was immediate and purely physical: find the source. I assumed it was a reflection of some lamp or a ray of sunlight, so I placed myself between the supposed source and the wood. Moving my arms, blocking angles, I did everything necessary to break the projection. But the orb remained there, static and unperturbed on the varnish. It was a moment of ontological vertigo: the light did not exist in that room, it had no body or origin in the three-dimensional space I occupied; it existed only in the reflection. It was an image, a light without an object. Yet, far from feeling fear, I was flooded with a calm curiosity, a silent respect. I felt it as foreign but harmless, like someone who discovers an exotic and very rare bird perched on a nearby branch and understands that trying to touch it or analyze it too much will only make it fly away. I decided not to force the physics. I slowly stepped away from the frame, letting it be, allowing it to exist in its small reality of wood and gleam.

**The Falling Sound** And again at home, shortly before my departure for the mountain—the mountain that would end up being the setting of the **RTM** corpus—another oddity occurred: one rainy night, lying in bed trying unsuccessfully to fall asleep, I heard the brutal sound of something massive falling from the sky at an impossible speed. A sharp, piercing whistle, as if an object were about to strike right above the building where I was. It was so physical that I did not think: I simply curled up in the fetal position, waiting for the impact. Nothing happened. No explosion, no tremor, no light. The night went on as before, and that subsequent normality was almost more disturbing than the sound. As if the event had occurred only on my channel.

I do not place these memories here to ask for anyone's faith. I place them here because, seen together, they explain my inclination: I grew up with an ambiguous relationship with the "possible." Not because I am special, but because I happened to live through enough cracks not to be entirely satisfied with easy explanations.

And that is where **Relatividad Temporal Multiescala** (**RTM**) comes in as a project. Not as a revelation, but as an attempt: the impulse to take an intuition (sometimes born of wonder, sometimes of fear, sometimes of beauty) and subject it to a discipline. To give it symbols. To ask it for its units. To force it to make predictions. If the framework holds, good. If it collapses, also good.

Thus ends this stretch of the story—or perhaps it is only beginning: from the scattered experiences that left me with unresolved questions to the decision to build a corpus that does not claim to close the mystery, but to make it legible and falsifiable.
